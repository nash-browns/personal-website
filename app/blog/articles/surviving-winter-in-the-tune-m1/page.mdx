## Power
Driving was almost always enough to handle my power needs. There were only a couple of times on the trip that I had to find a plug. Hotels in northern climates usually have plugs for block heaters. 

<TwoCenteredImages image={[battery, batteryCharger]} altText={['EcoFlow Delta Pro Battery', 'EcoFlow Delta Pro Battery Charger']} titles={['EcoFlow Battery Setup', 'EcoFlow Battery Charger Setup']} width={500} height={500} />

The issue happened when I flew home for Thanksgiving and accidentally left the lights on. This left the battery dead and cold. If the battery is too cold it won't charge. So I had to pull the battery and put it in the front seat as I drove and plugged it into the cigarette lighter charger. It eventually started taking a charge and I was able to find a plug at a community center to charge it the rest of the way.

There were other times the battery got too cold and would not charge. However, it will output power even if it's cold. So you can turn the heater on and it will warm the battery enough to charge. Also, even in negative temps, just running the heater while I slept every night was enough to keep the battery warm enough to charge.

All ways to charge:
- Driving
- Wall outlets
- EV Chargers (future)
- Solar (future): throw-out panels

Solar would also be pretty cool and I almost bought a panel to put on the top of the camper even though I already have a throw-out panel that I'll be adding to the setup this summer. I decided against it because I only wanted the panel if I was parked at a location for a few days without moving, which has been rare. The throw-out panels will work perfectly in that case while also being protected from the elements and portable. I just feel like a mounted panel will be sitting on the truck too much without being used. I don't have a strong opinion here. Having a panel constantly charging that doesn't require any setup would be pretty nice!

## Heater
The Truma heater worked great after a learning curve. I used the heater for comfort only. I woke up in Montana and it was -3 in the camper because I ran out of propane. Wool socks, smart wool leggings, and a sweatshirt combined with my bedding was plenty warm. However, getting out of bed is not fun at those temps! If you are looking for hard data on how the Truma performs in the Tune I recommend checking out my other article [Tune M1 Cold Weather Performance][25]!

<CenteredImage image={heater} altText={'Truma VarioHeat Heater'} title={'Truma VarioHeat'} width={1000} height={1000} />

### Initial Issue
When I got the heater it didn't work due to a loose fitting. It was a bit hard to track down because I didn't know what was normal for the heater. I tightened it up and haven't had any further issues. Just something to keep in mind. All of the common issues that I have run across while using the Tune can be found in this article: [Tune M1 Common Issues & Solutions][29].

### Propane Consumption
The biggest complaint that I have about the heater is that you can't tell how much fuel you have. I definitely had my fair share of cold mornings. Otherwise, it is great! Here is what I found:
- 2.5-4 nights per tank, highly dependent on how long you have it on, outdoor temp, and altitude
- I didn't track how long my "nights" were but 10 hours is probably a fair guess.

### Icing
There was a night in Wyoming that my heater quit. I woke up in the cold and was pretty confused because:
1. I had just filled the propane tank that day
2. It wasn't that cold, ~30 degrees

When I got out of the camper it had snowed about 10" and all the lines and regulators had about an 1/8 inch of ice on them. I assume this is why the propane stopped flowing. It also could have been that the regulator stopped working. This icing has only happened twice this winter. I think it takes a combo of snow, warm enough temps where the snow will melt on the roof, and a slight angle of the roof towards the propane tank. With all the factors in place, snow from the roof melts, drips on the regulator and lines, then refreezes causing an issue. Unfortunately, I was in a mood that morning and did not take any pictures, but the situation did happen again after I had implemented the fix. Here are some pictures to get an idea of the issue.

<ThreeCenteredImages image={[heaterIcicle3, heaterIcicle6, heaterIcicle5]} altText={['Ice on the propane regulator', 'Ice on the heater lines', 'Iced propane regulator']} titles={['Iced Regulator','Iced Lines', 'Iced Regulator']} width={300} height={300} />

The fix is to use [water pipe insulation][26] on the hoses and a [faucet cover][27] on the regulator. The second time there was icing on the lines and hoses was in South Dakota and the fix worked! Another benefit is that the faucet protector keeps the regulators from failing. I went through 3 regulators, but after using the faucet cover, I have had no issues. All three regulators that I used had a warning not to expose them to the elements printed on the side.

<TwoCenteredImages image={[heaterProtection, heaterHoseProtection]} altText={['Faucet Cover', 'Water Pipe Insulation']} titles={['Faucet Cover', 'Water Pipe Insulation']} width={500} height={500} />

### Condensation
When I bought the Tune, I knew that condensation was probably going to be an issue. After spending over 100 nights in the Tune I would say that it is and isn't. Here are a few things to keep in mind:

- You pretty much have to run the fan while you sleep even in the dry desert. The lowest setting seemed to work fine the entire time, but when it's really cold the heater and fan definitely are fighting a war.
- Aluminum is a great conductor and, if it's really cold, will collect a lot of condensation throughout the night.
- Anything that's wet is going to dry and the water will end up on the camper
	- swimsuits/towels, etc.
	- Try to keep these in the cab where it will just freeze.
- You don't really have to worry about mold, and it's easy to clean because there aren't traditional walls for it to hide in.

### Doing Laundry
The first laundromat that I went to was in Spokane, and the homeless dude next to me dumped his pockets and a crack pipe fell out. That was enough of big city laundries for me. Mountain towns have the best/cleanest laundromats. Doing laundry is actually super expensive. All my sheets and clothes could be upwards of $60. <CallOut title={'Laundry Hack'}>
The biggest hack I found in the laundry realm was the [laundry detergent that comes in sheets][30]. Huge game changer. Cheaper than single load detergent at the laundromat and it can be frozen! I kept these at the bottom of the dirty clothes bag. Keep reading to see why!
</CallOut>

<TwoCenteredImages image={[closet3, dirtyClothsStorage]} altText={['The closet bag', 'Dirty clothes storage']} titles={['The "Closet"', 'Dirty Clothes']} width={500} height={500} />

You can see my system above. My clean clothes are on the left, in the "closet." Dirty clothes are on the right. This keeps everything nice and separated, but the main benefit becomes apparent when you get to the laundromat.
1. You just pull up and grab the bag out of the back seat. You already have the detergent sheets at the bottom of the bag!
2. You throw the clothes in the wash, and return the bag to the truck.
3. When the clothes are done, you just take the "closet" bag in. Fold the clothes, throw them in the back, hook it back on the carabiner and you're heading down the road.

This is the best way to do laundry. Tell me I'm wrong!

I was able to accomplish this for about 80-100 dollars a night. That sounds crazy expensive, but by the time you factor in paying for parking, the possibility of your car getting broken into, showers, electricity, and laundry, it ends up being a pretty good deal.
